AI makes learning a new language faster than ever.

Fast enough to be a little dangerous.

Because it also makes believing you've learned it easier than ever.

Recently I used an AI agent to learn Go almost from scratch, from a real WebRTC project. I wrote that up as a series — if you want the concrete walkthrough, start with the [series primer](/en/blog/webrtc-poc-go-series-primer). But after a full pass, the lesson most worth writing down had almost nothing to do with Go. It was a trap I kept dodging the whole way.

I call it understanding debt.

## Understanding debt is technical debt for learning

Just like the technical debt every engineer knows:

When AI produces a chunk of code that runs but you can't read, you didn't save time — you took out a loan.

The interest comes due at the worst possible moment: usually when it breaks and you have no idea why.

Runnable is not the same as understood.

Understood is not the same as being able to judge where it will fail.

## The most dangerous part is that AI's explanations are too smooth

The sneakiest thing about this debt is how smooth AI's explanations are.

Learning science has a name for it: the fluency illusion. The more clearly and smoothly information is presented, the more we overestimate how much we've actually learned.

And AI happens to be about the smoothest explainer ever built.

So the first thing I learned was this: the more it all looks obvious and the harder you're nodding along, the more you should force yourself to stop and ask —

**"If I closed the AI right now, could I explain this myself?"**

## Three moves that pay the debt down

If that question is the alarm, these three moves are what actually pays the debt down. They showed up again and again while I learned Go; there's a version closer to the code in the [series wrap-up](/en/blog/active-learning-new-language-with-ai-agent), so here I'll just say why they work.

**Ask smaller, not more.**
I almost never ask "teach me all of Go." I ask "why does this use `package main`," or "why doesn't this `rooms map` need a lock." AI defaults to feeding you answers; it won't slow you down on its own. Making the question smaller is you supplying the discipline it won't.

**Get it wrong first, then let it correct you.**
I state my version — "here's what I think this does" — in my own words first, then let the AI poke holes in where I'd actually confused things. That "oh, that's where I had it wrong" moment is worth more than seeing the right answer first. Memory keeps the parts you generate yourself, not the parts you scroll past.

**Take the answer back to reality and validate it.**
Run the server, read the logs, break the connection on purpose and watch how it reacts. Understanding that lives only in a chat is floating; understanding calibrated against execution is what stands up.

## What's scarce has changed hands

In the end, this taught me something bigger.

Answers used to be scarce — you had to dig through books, ask seniors, fall into your own pits. Now answers are infinite and arrive instantly. So the scarce thing has changed hands.

What's scarce now is judgement: judging whether an answer is right, whether it fits where you are right now, and whether you actually understand it.

In an era where producing plausible-looking content is nearly free, being able to validate and judge is the one moat no one can take from you.

## In the end

I only set out to learn Go. What I came away with was a posture better suited to this era:

Start from a real problem, let AI break it into smaller pieces and speed up the feedback — but keep the final call, "do I actually understand this," firmly in your own hands.

Don't resist AI, and don't deify it. Just put it back where it belongs: a powerful amplifier, not a replacement for understanding.

If I had to compress it to one line:

**Let AI help you find the entrance, then make that entrance into understanding yourself.**

Next time you learn something with AI, it might be worth pausing to ask: this time, did I actually understand it — or did I just take on a little more understanding debt?
