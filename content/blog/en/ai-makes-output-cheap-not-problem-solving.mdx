Something I've been feeling lately.

Some clients wonder: now that AI helps engineers write code faster, shouldn't the price of software development be able to come down a bit too?

Honestly, I understand the thought. On the surface, AI really has made a lot of things faster.

Engineers can write code faster. Designers can produce drafts faster. Marketers can pull together copy faster. Consultants can build decks faster. Product teams can spin up an early prototype faster.

Things that used to take a lot of time to organise, search for, iterate on, format, and correct — AI can now move a big chunk of that forward for us.

So if you only look at "speed of output," it's easy to land on an intuition: if it's done faster, shouldn't it be cheaper?

But the more I thought about it, the more I felt this can't be the only way to look at it.

## What clients buy was never the deliverable itself

Because whether it's software, design, content, consulting, or any other professional work, what clients are really buying usually isn't the "deliverable" itself.

Not a few snippets of code. Not a few design mockups. Not a few slides. Not a few articles. And not a draft that merely looks complete.

What's truly valuable is usually whether, behind those outputs, something actually happened:

- Was the problem clarified?
- Was the process improved?
- Did the decision become clearer?
- Was the risk reduced?
- Did communication cost go down?
- Did users actually adopt it?
- And in the end, did it produce a real effect?

These are things AI won't do for you automatically.

## AI speeds up output, but doesn't automatically understand the context

AI can quickly write you a piece of code, but it won't automatically know whether that code, once dropped into your existing system, will become a maintenance headache six months later.

AI can produce a version of a design, but it won't automatically know whether that design truly fits how users behave.

AI can put together a deck for you, but it won't automatically know whether that deck can persuade the people who actually need persuading.

AI can generate an article, but it won't automatically know whether it actually speaks to the question on the reader's mind.

So what AI really changes, I think, isn't that "value disappeared." It's that AI lets us see more clearly: output and value are not the same thing.

## We're used to measuring value by what's visible

In a lot of work situations, we actually already knew that the outcome is what matters.

It's just that in practice, when value is hard to quantify, we often borrow more visible indicators to judge by. For example:

- How much time was spent
- How many people were involved
- How many features were completed
- How many pages of documents were produced
- How many revisions were made
- How many visible things were delivered

These indicators aren't necessarily wrong — they do reflect part of the cost, effort, and workload. But they don't necessarily equal real value.

AI's arrival just makes this more obvious. When the output itself becomes faster and easier, we need even more to step back and ask:

- What problem did this output actually solve?
- Did it make things better?
- Did it lower cost, reduce errors, improve the process?
- Did it make it easier for the team to understand, execute, or decide?
- Was it actually used, rather than just delivered?

If not, then no matter how complete it looks, it's just "a piece of output."

But if it did, then even if it was accelerated by AI, it still has value.

## The real value is in the "conversion"

So I wouldn't say: "AI made professional services cheap."

I'd rather say:

**AI is making output that has no context, no judgement, and no conversion behind it cheaper and cheaper.**

What's truly valuable is whether you can turn what AI gives you into an outcome that can be understood, used, executed — and even produce results.

That conversion in the middle is where human value lies.

AI can speed up output. But it won't complete the value conversion for you.

## What sets people apart next is judgement

And what truly sets people apart going forward may not be who's better at generating content, images, code, or slides.

It's who's better at judging:

- What's worth doing
- What shouldn't be done
- What only looks reasonable
- What doesn't actually fit the real situation
- What will become a cost later
- What will actually solve the problem

So AI may change the price structure of a lot of work. But it shouldn't let us measure value only by "getting it done faster."

Because what's truly worth paying for was never just the speed of output.

It's the change that comes after the problem is actually solved.
