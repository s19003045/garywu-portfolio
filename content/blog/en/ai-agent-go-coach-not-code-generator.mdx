Go is still a new language to me.

This series is not meant to be a full Go course. It is a practical learning record built around a real WebRTC project and a simple question: how should an engineer learn a new language in the age of AI agents?

If I had approached this the old way, I probably would have started with a Go book, a tutorial series, and a gradual path through syntax, examples, and toy exercises. There is nothing wrong with that approach. It is still valuable. But it usually costs a lot of front-loaded time, and it can feel far away from the way engineers actually meet new languages at work.

In real projects, the codebase is already there. The problem is already there. The delivery pressure is already there. What I often need is not complete mastery on day one. I need a reliable way to build enough understanding to read, reason, debug, and keep moving.

That is why I wanted this series to be about more than Go itself. I wanted it to capture a way of learning with AI that stays active, deliberate, and grounded in real code.

## Who this series is for

This series is probably most useful if you are in one of these situations:

- you already work as an engineer, but Go is still unfamiliar to you
- you want to learn from a real codebase instead of isolated examples
- you already use AI agents, but do not want to treat them as pure code factories
- you want to see how questioning, verification, and debugging can become part of the learning process itself

If what you want is a full Go curriculum, this will not replace one.

If what you want is a realistic, repeatable learning path for a new language in modern engineering work, that is much closer to what this series is trying to offer.

## What this article is about

This opening article sets up the method behind the series:

- why I did not start from a traditional Go textbook
- why I used an AI agent as a programming coach
- what I expect from this learning process
- what readers should and should not expect from the series

At the center of it is one idea: AI changes the entry point, but it does not remove the learner's responsibility.

It can give me runnable code faster.
It can give me a first-pass explanation faster.
It can point me to the most relevant files faster.

But it cannot place understanding in my head for me.

## Why I did not want to start with a textbook

Traditional learning paths usually optimise for completeness.

That has clear benefits, but it also creates friction for working engineers. When a language is new, the first thing I often need is not complete coverage. I need orientation.

I need to know:

- where the program starts
- which files matter first
- who owns state
- how data moves
- which concept I should understand next

AI agents are very good at helping with that first layer of orientation. They can reduce the distance between “I have no entry point” and “I can now ask a precise question about a real piece of code.”

That shift matters a lot.

## What I want to keep from the process

I do not only want to preserve polished conclusions.

I also want to preserve:

- where I misunderstood a concept at first
- how I asked follow-up questions
- how the AI corrected my mental model
- how I verified my understanding with code and logs

That process matters, because the value is not only in getting an answer. It is in turning the answer into understanding.

That is also why I do not want to simply paste raw AI conversations into articles.

What matters is not the full transcript. What matters is the part where understanding actually changed:

- why I got stuck
- what I asked next
- what was still too vague in the first answer
- what became clearer after correction and validation

If the process stays visible, the article becomes more than a conclusion. It becomes a learning model other people can reuse.

## How I am using the AI agent in this series

In this project, I am not treating the AI as an automatic answer machine.

I am using it more like a programming coach.

That means I ask it to do things like:

- identify the smallest useful reading entry point
- explain one local concept in the context of the real project
- correct my wording when my understanding is directionally right but imprecise
- turn control flow into an event timeline
- suggest the cheapest validation step after a code or understanding change

That is a very different posture from simply saying “write the code for me.”

It also changes what I need to do as the learner. I have to answer back. I have to restate what I think I understood. I have to validate it. I have to notice when I am only half-convinced.

That is where the real learning happens.

One point I'll expand on in the final article of the series: the "AI" here isn't only the agent inside the IDE. Throughout the process I also kept Gemini, Claude, and ChatGPT open to cross-check the same concept from different angles, and went back to the official [A Tour of Go](https://go.dev/tour/list) to calibrate. When you learn a new language, resources are never the scarce part — the hard part is filtering down to the few that fit you. I'll come back to that in the [final article](/en/blog/active-learning-new-language-with-ai-agent).

## What this series is, and what it is not

This series is:

- a practical record of learning Go from a real WebRTC signaling server
- a study of how to use an AI agent as a coach rather than a substitute thinker
- a way of connecting language concepts back to architecture, logs, and runtime behavior

This series is not:

- a full Go reference
- a textbook-style introduction to every part of the language
- a dump of unedited AI conversation transcripts
- a detached collection of syntax notes with no system context

That distinction matters, because I want readers to know what kind of value to expect here.

## What the rest of the series will cover

The later articles will move through seven practical steps:

1. learning from a real project entry point
2. understanding goroutines, channels, and the Hub model
3. reading the lifecycle of a WebSocket connection
4. following join, signal, and leave event flow
5. using minimal logs to debug a Go service
6. extracting a repeatable learning method from the whole process

Each article is meant to do two things at once:

1. explain one concrete Go or systems concept through a real project
2. show how that concept became understandable through AI-guided interaction, correction, and validation

## The question behind the whole series

If I had to reduce the whole project to one question, it would be this:

**In the age of AI agents, how do we learn a new programming language more actively, instead of merely receiving answers?**

That question is larger than Go.

It applies to new frameworks, unfamiliar infrastructure, and inherited codebases. Go just happens to be the concrete training ground for this series.
