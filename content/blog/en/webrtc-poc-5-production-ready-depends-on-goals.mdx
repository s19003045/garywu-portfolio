After finishing the multi-node SFU cluster in Phase 6, a harder question surfaced for me:

**Is this "ready to ship"?**

Honestly, the answer is "it depends." And that "it depends" is not a dodge — it is the nature of this kind of system. What I want to record here is not how to build a particular piece of tech, but a judgment I think matters more: **the distance from POC to product depends on your goals; different goals lead to completely different architectures.**

If you have not seen how this project went from 1:1 to a multi-node SFU, start with the [previous article](/en/blog/webrtc-poc-4-sfu-scale).

## "Is it done" is the wrong question

It is easy to imagine "production-ready" as a fixed checklist: tick every box, then ship.

But video systems do not work that way. A system "for 5-person small-group teaching" and a system "for a 10,000-person concert livestream" share almost no architecture. Applying the first one's checklist to the second is simply wrong.

So the right question is not "is it done," but:

**For whom, in what scenario, at what scale, under what constraints — and therefore, how far do I actually need to go.**

Only after answering that does the technology choice have a basis. Otherwise it easily becomes "I know SFU, so let's use SFU for everything" — the classic "when you have a hammer, everything looks like a nail."

## three scenarios I use to interrogate myself

To make "it depends" concrete, I picked three very different scenarios that the next two articles will keep comparing:

1. **An open large-scale video system (like Google Meet)**: many people, many rooms, a per-room cap, everyone turning on their camera and interacting.
2. **A security-strict multinational enterprise (so strict that personal devices are banned from the office)**: not necessarily many users, but data must never leak, must comply with multiple countries' laws, and must be auditable.
3. **A 10,000-person concert livestream**: a single "room" with tens of thousands of people, but the vast majority only watch; only a few on stage are sending.

You will see these three pull every decision axis in a different direction.

## the axes that actually decide the architecture

I break "how far do I need to go" into a few axes I can answer one by one:

- **The "shape" of scale**: is it "many small-to-medium rooms," or "a single enormous room"? This matters more than "how many people total."
- **Interactive vs broadcast**: does everyone publish, or do a few publish while many watch? This decides the media model.
- **Build vs adopt**: do you maintain the media layer yourself, or use LiveKit / SaaS? This is the trade-off between control, cost, and time-to-market.
- **Data and compliance**: can data live in the public cloud? Do you need on-prem, end-to-end encryption, audit logs?
- **Time-to-market vs long-term control**: fast first, or stable first? This loops back and influences every axis above.

The point: these axes have **no universal best answer**; each one is decided by the scenario.

## same axes, three different answers

Run the three scenarios through these axes and the divergence appears immediately:

| Axis | Open large-scale (Meet-like) | Security-strict enterprise | 10k concert |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape of scale | Many small/medium rooms (A) | Small/medium rooms, highly sensitive | Single huge room (B) |
| Interactive vs broadcast | Mostly interactive | Mostly interactive | Mostly broadcast |
| Build vs adopt | Adopt (LiveKit/SaaS) | Self-host / on-prem (no public-cloud SaaS) | Broadcast pipeline + a little SFU |
| Data and compliance | Standard | Highest: on-prem, E2EE, audit | Medium: licensing, ticketing |
| Biggest cost | Node cost at scale | Compliance and operations | CDN traffic |

Same team, same knowledge — change only the goal, and the best strategy changes entirely. That is exactly what I want these articles to make clear.

## derive from goals, do not start from the tech

The biggest takeaway from this is methodological:

**Spell out the goals and constraints first, and let them pick the technology; do not fall in love with a technology first and then look for scenarios it fits.**

Having self-built up to Phase 6, I felt a pull: since I already wrote an SFU cluster myself, surely I should self-build for production too. But once the goals are laid out, you see that for "Meet-like" and "enterprise," build-vs-adopt land on opposite answers; and "concert" should not put everyone into WebRTC at all.

The value of self-building is not that you will "always self-build." It is that you can **see what problem each option solves**, so when a real choice arrives, you can judge instead of believe.

## glossary

- **SFU**: Selective Forwarding Unit, a server that receives and forwards media (common for interactive multi-party video).
- **Broadcast pipeline**: converting media into a stream like HLS/LL-HLS and letting a CDN distribute it to a huge audience.
- **LiveKit**: an open-source SFU built on Pion, with built-in multi-node, simulcast, recording, and SDKs.
- **E2EE**: end-to-end encryption, where even the server cannot see the media content.
